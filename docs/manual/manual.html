Nyahmation is a studio for making cartoons. You draw a character (or import one), connect its parts at joints like a paper puppet, pose it on the frames that matter, and Nyahmation fills in every frame in between. Add a voice, let Nyahmation (or you) match the mouth shapes to it, point a camera at the scene, and export a video you can play anywhere.

The big idea: poses, not drawings

Think of a flip book. Drawing every page by hand is a lot of work. In Nyahmation you only set up the important pages, called poses: the arm down on frame 1, the arm up on frame 12. Nyahmation draws the 10 pages in between for you, smoothly. Want the move faster? Slide the second pose closer to the first. That's the whole trick, and the rest of this manual builds on it.

There is no difference between a "keyframe" and a normal frame: you can pose on any frame at any time, and only the parts you actually move get a pose. If you wave a hand on frame 20, the legs aren't affected.

A puppet on a stage, filmed by a camera

It helps to picture three separate things, because Nyahmation keeps them separate too:

  • The puppets (characters) and the scenery (backgrounds) stand on a stage. Posing moves them around the stage.
  • The camera films the stage. Moving the camera changes what ends up in the picture, but never moves a puppet. A foot pinned to the floor stays pinned however the camera swoops.
  • The soundtrack runs alongside. Nothing you do to the picture ever shifts the sound, so the lip sync you match to it stays matched.

When something surprises you, asking "am I moving the puppet, the camera, or the sound?" usually explains it.

Try it first. Choose File → Open Demo Puppet. It opens Pip, a ready-made character who waves and talks. Poke around: you can't break anything, and ⌘Z undoes any change. Have fun, and make something that makes someone laugh.

Three steps to a cartoon

  1. Build. Draw your character as separate parts (head, body, arms, legs) and set up where each part bends. This is Build mode.
  2. Animate. Switch to Animate mode, pick a frame, and pose. Add sound and lip sync, and move the camera.
  3. Export. Save it as a video, with the sound.

Opening Nyahmation

Once Nyahmation is installed in Applications, open it like any other app: from Launchpad, the Dock, or by double-clicking it. Double-clicking a saved project (a .nyah file) opens it too.

Installing it on a Mac (for grown-ups)

Nyahmation isn't in the App Store; you make the app from its source code once. You need Node.js (the LTS version from nodejs.org) and a copy of the code (from GitHub: pick the branch, then Code → Download ZIP). Then, in Terminal, inside the Nyahmation folder:

npm install
npm run dist:mac

After 5–10 minutes a Finder window shows Nyahmation-0.0.1.dmg. Double-click it and drag Nyahmation onto Applications. That's it: from now on nobody needs Terminal.

If macOS blocks it the first time ("can't check it for malicious software"), click Done, then open System Settings → Privacy & Security, scroll down, and click Open Anyway. This happens because the app isn't signed with a paid Apple developer account; it's only needed once.

To try it without installing, run npm run dev in the same folder instead. The app opens and runs until you close Terminal.

Automatic lip sync needs one extra download

npm install also fetches Rhubarb Lip Sync, the free program that listens to dialogue for Auto lip sync (). It's about 90 MB and needs the internet once. If that download was skipped or failed, run npm run setup in the Nyahmation folder, and build the app again. Everything else works without it. On a Mac with Apple Silicon, Rhubarb runs through Rosetta; if macOS offers to install Rosetta, say yes.

Several people on one Mac

Apps in Applications are shared by every account on the Mac, so each person finds Nyahmation in their own Launchpad. Each person's projects and library stay in their own account. The first time someone saves to the library, macOS asks whether Nyahmation may use their Documents folder: click Allow.

What you need

  • A Mac with a recent version of macOS (Intel or Apple Silicon), or a Windows 10/11 PC. A 2020 MacBook Air is plenty.
  • No internet connection, account or subscription. Everything stays on your computer.

Moving around the canvas

  • Pan: scroll, or hold Space and drag, or use the Hand tool (H).
  • Zoom: pinch on the trackpad, or hold ⌘ and scroll. Fit shows the whole scene.
  • The light rectangle is the scene: exactly what ends up in the video. You can draw outside it, but that won't be exported (unless the camera looks there; see ).

Everything in Nyahmation is drawn with vector shapes: outlines made of points and curves. They stay perfectly sharp at any size, and you can reshape them at any time.

The drawing tools

ToolKeyHow to use it
SelectVClick a part to select it, and drag to move it. Drag the square handles to resize (⇧ keeps the proportions, ⌥ resizes from the centre) and the round handle to rotate (⇧ snaps to 15°). Drag on an empty spot to select everything in a box. Double-click a shape to edit its points.
PointsAReshape a shape by moving its points, their handles, or the curves between them.
PenPClick to place corner points; click and drag to make curves. Click the first point to close the shape. Enter or a double-click finishes an open line; Backspace removes the last point.
RectangleMDrag out a rectangle. Set rounded corners in the tool options.
EllipseLDrag out an oval. Hold ⇧ for a circle.
PolygonYDrag out a shape with equal sides. Choose how many sides in the tool options.
StarSDrag out a star. Choose the number of points and how deep the dents are.
Line\Drag a straight line. ⇧ keeps it at 45° steps.
HandHDrag to move your view of the canvas.

For all the shape tools, hold ⇧ to keep the shape even (a square, a circle) and ⌥ to draw from the centre out.

Colours and outlines

With a shape selected, the Fill & stroke section in Properties sets its colours. Untick Fill or Stroke to leave it out. The settings you choose are also used for the next shapes you draw. Holes decides what happens where a shape overlaps itself (for example, the middle of a letter O).

  • Eyedropper. The pipette button beside each colour picks a colour from anywhere on the screen: another shape, a reference picture, even another app. Esc cancels.
  • Swatches are the project's own palette, saved with it, so a character's skin and shirt colours are always one click away. Click a swatch to use it for the fill, ⇧-click for the outline, and ⌥-click to remove it. + adds the current fill colour.

Gradients

Set Fill type to Gradient (straight) for colours that fade from one side to the other (a sunset, a shiny tube) or Gradient (round) for colours that fade from the middle outwards (a glowing sun, a ball). Pick the two colours below it, and for a straight gradient its Angle. Gradients in imported SVG files are kept too.

Editing points

  • Drag a point, a curve, or a point's handles to reshape the shape.
  • Double-click a curve to add a point; double-click a point to switch it between smooth (a round curve through it) and sharp (a corner).
  • Hold ⌥ while dragging a handle to bend just one side of a smooth point.
  • Delete removes the selected points. Esc stops editing.

Arranging

  • In front or behind: Object → Bring Forward / Send Backward (⌘] / ⌘[), and Bring to Front / Send to Back with ⇧.
  • Group (⌘G) keeps several parts together so they move as one. Combine Shapes (⌘8) melts the selected shapes into a single shape. To merge, cut or overlap outlines instead, use the Shape Tools below.
  • Nudge the selection with the arrow keys (add ⇧ for 10 pixels at a time).

Shape tools: making new shapes from overlaps

Select two or more overlapping shapes in Build mode and choose one of the Object → Shape Tools. Think of cookie dough and cutters:

ToolKeyWhat you get
Union⌥⌘UOne shape covering all of them, like pressing lumps of dough together. Good for a cloud made of circles.
Subtract⌥⌘SThe back shape with the front shapes cut out of it, like a cookie cutter. Good for a crescent moon or a bite out of an apple.
Intersect⌥⌘KOnly the part where they all overlap. Good for a lens or a leaf.
Exclude⌥⌘EEverything except where they overlap; the overlaps become holes.

The result keeps the back shape's name, colours and effects, and its curves stay smooth and editable with the Points tool. It's one undo step.

Cut, copy and paste

In Build mode, Edit → Cut, Copy and Paste (⌘X, ⌘C, ⌘V) work on the selected parts, or on a whole layer if its row is selected. Pasted parts land 20 pixels down and to the right of the last paste, inside the selected group or the active layer. You can paste into another project too: copy, open the other project, paste. Mouths bring their drawings with them. Animation isn't copied with parts; copy poses in Animate mode instead ().

Typing? While you're typing in a text field, the same keys cut, copy and paste the text instead.

Importing art

File → Import Art or Sound… (⌘I) brings in:

  • SVG files from other drawing apps (Inkscape, Illustrator, Affinity, Figma…). Each layer or group in the file becomes a part, with its name, so a character drawn elsewhere arrives ready to rig. Gradients come along. Things Nyahmation can't use, such as text, are listed in a message.
  • PNG and JPEG pictures. PNGs can have see-through backgrounds. Pictures have a fixed number of pixels, so they look soft if you blow them up; Nyahmation warns you when you export.
  • Sounds: see .

A scene is a stack of layers. Each layer holds parts, and parts can hold other parts. The Layers panel shows all of it, front to back.

Two kinds of layer

LayerUse it for
Character (C)One character per layer. Its parts can be rigged and posed, and the whole character can be saved to the library.
Background (B)Scenery: sky, hills, a room. It sits behind the characters (drag it up the list to put it in front, for things like a table the character stands behind). Backgrounds can have depth, scroll, and stay fixed to the camera ().

The part tree

A part inside another part moves with it, like a hand attached to a forearm attached to an upper arm. This is what makes a puppet: move the upper arm and everything below it comes along.

  • Put a part inside another: drag its row onto the other part's row.
  • Reorder: drag a row onto the top or bottom edge of another row.
  • Rename: double-click a name. Good names help: they appear on the timeline, mouth shapes are recognised by their names, and Mirror Pose pairs parts by their names ().
  • Hide with the eye, lock with the padlock. A locked part can't be selected, and nothing can be drawn on a locked layer.
  • Select the parent of the selected part with ⇧Enter.
  • Copy a whole layer (a character with all its parts) by selecting its row and pressing ⌘C, then ⌘V, here or in another project.

Tip. Clicking on the canvas selects the exact part under the mouse (the hand, not the whole arm). Use the Layers panel, or ⇧Enter, to select something bigger.

What's in front

Inside a character, which part is drawn in front is separate from the part tree. That means a back arm can be inside the torso (so it moves with it) and still be drawn behind it. Use Bring Forward and Send Backward to change it. In Animate mode the same commands change the order from the current frame on, for an arm that swings behind the body ().

Rigging means telling Nyahmation where each part bends: the shoulder, the elbow, the knee. It takes a few minutes and then posing is a matter of dragging.

Rigging a character, step by step

  1. Put the character on a character layer, drawn as separate parts: head, torso, upper arm, forearm, hand, and so on.
  2. In the Layers panel, build the tree: drag each hand onto its forearm, each forearm onto its upper arm, each upper arm onto the torso, and the head onto the torso.
  3. Choose the Joints tool (J) and drag each part's joint to where it attaches: the shoulder, elbow, wrist, neck, hips and knees. The drawing stays put; only the turning point moves.
  4. Select the character's layer and click Mark branch joints as chain roots in Properties (or use the Object menu). This stops a dragged hand from tipping the whole body over.
  5. Try it with the Pose tool.

Joint settings

SettingWhat it does
PositionThe exact position of the joint.
Chain rootPosing stops at this joint. Double-clicking a joint with the Joints tool does the same; chain roots are drawn as squares.
LimitsStop a joint turning too far, so an elbow can't bend backwards. Set the smallest and largest angle.
BendsWhich way a straight arm or leg bends first when you pull on it (clockwise or counter-clockwise).

Posing with the Pose tool

With the Pose tool (K), drag any part and the parts it's attached to bend to follow, the way a real arm does. This is called inverse kinematics (IK). Hover over a part first to see which joints will turn. In Build mode, posing sets the character's resting pose; in Animate mode, it records a pose on the current frame.

Drag with…What happens
Nothing heldThe parents bend to follow (the elbow and shoulder turn when you drag the hand).
⇧ ShiftThe part turns at its own joint only (just the hand turns at the wrist).
⌥ OptionThe part moves away from its joint.
⌘ CommandThe whole character moves.

The library is your collection of reusable characters, backgrounds and shapes, shared by all your projects. It's a folder of files in Documents/Nyahmation Library.

Saving to the library

  1. Select a character's layer (click its row in the Layers panel), or some parts.
  2. Open the Library tab and click Save to library…
  3. Give it a name and some tags (words to find it by, separated by commas), and click Save. A picture is made automatically.

A background keeps its depth, scrolling and fixed-to-camera settings in the library ().

Using a library item

Click Add. The project gets its own copy, so you can change it freely without changing the library, and you can add the same item as many times as you like (a crowd of Pips!). Shapes land where they were when you saved them.

Updating a character everywhere

Say Pip gets a new hat. The projects that already use Pip each have their own copy, so they don't change by themselves; you decide when they do. Think of it like a recipe card: each project baked its own cake from the card, and updating the card doesn't change cakes already baked until you bake again.

  1. Improve the character in any project, select its layer, and click Save to library…. Because the layer came from the library, the form offers Save as a new version of “Pip the puppet” (replaces it), already ticked. Click Save.
  2. In another project that uses Pip, select Pip's layer. Properties show where it came from, and say when the library has a newer version.
  3. Click Update from library. The new drawings and rig come in, and the animation is kept: every part that still exists keeps its poses. The status bar says how many parts kept their animation, and how many are new or gone.

Organising. Click Folder to open the library in Finder. Make subfolders there, such as Mouths or Characters/Animals, and move items into them; the Library tab shows the folders. Click ↻ after making changes in Finder.

Switch to Animate mode with the button at the top. The timeline appears at the bottom of the window.

Making your first animation

  1. Open the demo puppet, or your own rigged character, and switch to Animate.
  2. Click frame 12 on the timeline's number row (or press → eleven times).
  3. Choose the Pose tool (K) and drag the hand up. A ◆ appears on the rows of the parts that moved, on frame 12.
  4. The first time a part moves, its starting position is also kept on frame 1 automatically, so the arm moves from where it was.
  5. Press Space to play. The arm rises smoothly from frame 1 to frame 12.
  6. Go to frame 24 and pose the hand down again. Now it waves up and down.

How poses work

  • Only the parts you change get a pose. The rest keep doing whatever they were doing.
  • Between two poses, the motion is smooth: it speeds up and slows down naturally and flows through poses instead of stopping at each one.
  • You can also pose by typing numbers in Properties (position, rotation, scale, opacity, and effect settings), or with the Select tool. Any change on a frame becomes a pose.

The timeline rows

Playing it back

  • Space plays and pauses. ← → step one frame; ⇧← ⇧→ jump to the previous or next pose. Home and End go to the start and end (on a MacBook keyboard: Fn ← and Fn →).
  • Loop range: press I on the first frame and O on the last. Playback repeats just that part, which is handy while you polish one move. × clears it.
  • Drag the top edge of the timeline to make it taller, and use − / + to zoom it.

Smooth playback on a slower computer

While a scene plays, the timeline bar shows Showing 24 of 24 fps: how many frames a second your computer is actually drawing. If it turns orange (say, Showing 15 of 24 fps), some frames are being skipped to keep up with the sound. The picture stays in time; it's just less smooth. Effects such as blur are the usual cause.

  • Preview in the top bar draws the canvas at Full, Half or Quarter resolution. Lower is softer but much faster, like squinting at the screen. Handles and outlines stay sharp. Nyahmation remembers the choice on this computer.
  • View → Measure Preview Speed times your scene at each quality and recommends one.

Exports are never affected. The Preview setting only changes what you see while working. Exported videos always draw every frame at full quality, however long that takes.

Changing what's in front, over time

An arm that swings behind the body needs to change its place in the stacking order partway through the move. In Animate mode, select the part, go to the frame where it should pass behind, and choose Send Backward (⌘[) or Send to Back (⇧⌘[); Bring Forward and Bring to Front go the other way. A mark on the part's row records the change, and the new order holds until the next change. Drag the mark to retime it like any other pose.

Scene settings

With nothing selected (press Esc), Properties shows the scene: its size in pixels (1920 × 1080 is standard HD video), the frame rate (24, 25, 30 or 60 frames a second; 24 is the classic cartoon rate), the length in frames, the background colour, an optional Sky gradient (), and Animate on ().

Retiming: making moves faster or slower

Timing is what makes animation feel alive, and in Nyahmation it's just dragging:

  • Drag a ◆ to another frame. A pose on frame 10 dragged to frame 5 makes that move twice as fast.
  • ⇧-drag moves the pose and everything after it on that row, so the timing of the rest of the scene is kept.
  • ⌥-drag (or Ctrl-drag) copies the pose to another frame. Two copies of the same pose make a hold: the character stays still between them.
  • Click a ◆ to select it (⇧-click for more), then press Delete to remove it.
  • Which row you drag on decides what moves: the Scene row moves every part's pose on that frame (and the camera's), a layer row moves one character's, and a part row moves just that part's.

Lip sync stays put. Dragging on the Scene or a layer row never moves the mouth shapes, because the voice they match doesn't move. To shift the mouth, drag on the Mouth's own row.

Stretching a whole stretch of animation

Dragging poses one by one is fine for one move. To make a whole passage slower or faster, set a loop range around it (I and O), then type a new length into Stretch to … frames on the timeline bar. The poses inside spread out or squeeze together evenly, like stretching a spring, and every pose after the range moves along so the rest keeps its timing.

  • With parts selected, only their animation is stretched (and the parts inside them). With nothing selected, the whole scene and the camera are.
  • Lip sync isn't stretched unless you select the mouth, because the dialogue doesn't change speed.

Easing: how a move starts and stops

Select a pose and choose Motion out in Properties. It decides how the move from this pose to the next one travels:

ChoiceLooks like
SmoothThe default. Flows naturally through the poses.
Ease in and outStarts slowly, speeds up, and settles gently. Good for a move that starts and stops.
Ease inStarts slowly, ends fast. Good for a fall or a throw.
Ease outStarts fast, ends slowly. Good for landing or coming to rest.
LinearThe same speed all the way. Mechanical things.
HoldDoesn't move at all, then jumps to the next pose. Snappy, cartoony changes.
Custom curve…Your own timing, drawn as a curve (below).

Drawing your own timing curve

The curve is a graph of the move: time runs left to right, and how far the part has got runs bottom to top. Steep means fast; flat means slow. Drag the two handles to bend it. Push a handle above the top for an overshoot (the arm swings a little past its pose and settles back) or below the bottom for an anticipation (a little wind-up the other way first, like crouching before a jump). The presets Gentle, Snappy, Slow start, Overshoot and Anticipate are good starting points.

On ones, twos and threes

Classic hand-drawn cartoons often show each drawing for two frames ("on twos"), which gives a lively, drawn feel. Set Animate on in the scene's Properties to Ones, Twos or Threes, or change it for just one character in its layer's Properties. Mouths always stay on ones, so lip sync stays accurate, and so does the camera.

  • Changing it partway through. Fast action often looks best on ones, and the calm bits on twos. In Animate mode, select a character's layer, go to the frame where the change should happen, and pick Animate on in its Properties. A mark on the layer's row records it, and it lasts until the next change.
  • View on ones. When anything is on twos or threes, a View on ones checkbox appears on the timeline bar. Tick it to see every frame smoothly while you work on the motion; the export still uses twos and threes.

Onion skin

Tick Onion skin on the timeline to see faint copies of nearby frames: red for earlier, green for later. It shows the path a movement takes, so you can check that an arm swings in a nice arc.

Pins: feet that stay on the floor

When a character walks or crouches, its feet should stay planted instead of sliding. That's what pins are for.

  1. Go to the frame where the foot touches the ground.
  2. Choose the Pin tool (P) and click the foot. It's pinned from this frame on.
  3. Move the body in later frames: the foot stays exactly where it is, and the leg bends to reach it.
  4. On the frame where the foot should lift, click it again with the Pin tool to release it.

The yellow bar on the part's row shows the frames where it's pinned. Pins hold the foot to the stage, so the camera can move all it likes without the foot sliding.

A walk is the classic animation exercise, and three tools make it much quicker: copying poses, mirroring them, and repeating a stretch of animation as a cycle. They're useful well beyond walks, too: a blink every few seconds, a flag flapping, a dog wagging its tail.

Copying and pasting poses

In Animate mode, Copy (⌘C) copies the pose of the selected parts on this frame (select the character's layer row to copy the whole character). Then:

  • Paste on another frame (⌘V) to repeat the pose later, for a hold or for the end of a cycle.
  • Paste onto another character by selecting it first. Parts are matched by name, so this works between copies of the same character (or any character with the same part names).

Mouth shapes aren't included, because they belong to the dialogue.

Mirror Pose and Swap Sides

Both work in Animate mode on the selected parts, or on the whole active character if nothing is selected, and record poses on the current frame. They need to know which parts are a pair, and they find out from the names: left/right, front/back, or a lone L/R ("Arm L", "leg_R"). Parts inside a pair pair up too, so "Hand" inside "Forearm (front)" pairs with "Hand (back)" inside "Forearm (back)".

CommandKeyWhat it does
Mirror Pose⌥⌘MFlips the pose side to side, like looking in a mirror: the left arm takes the right arm's pose turned the other way, and parts without a partner (the head, the torso) turn the other way themselves. For characters facing the camera: wave with the other hand in one keystroke.
Swap Sides⌥⇧⌘MPaired parts swap poses exactly as they are; everything else stays. For characters seen from the side, this is the second half of a walk: the left leg does what the right leg just did.

Cycles: repeating animation

A cycle is like a record's groove: the needle goes round the same loop again and again. Select a character's (or background's) layer, tick Repeat frames in its Properties, and set From and To. If you've set a loop range, the cycle starts with it. After frame To, the layer plays frames From to To again, for the rest of the scene.

  • Frame To counts as frame From of the next repeat, so pose them the same (copy and paste the pose). Otherwise the character jumps at every repeat.
  • Keep moving makes each repeat start where the last one ended. The character's top part and anything pinned move on by the distance one cycle covers, so a walk actually walks, with its feet still planted.
  • Lip sync is never repeated: mouth shapes follow the dialogue. Everything else in the layer repeats, including blinks, draw-order changes, pins and changes of Animate on.

A walk in six steps

  1. On frame 1, pose the first contact: the front foot's heel just touching the floor, the back foot's toe about to leave it, arms swinging opposite to the legs.
  2. Copy the pose (⌘C), go to frame 13, paste (⌘V) and choose Swap Sides (⌥⇧⌘M). That's the second contact, with the other leg forward.
  3. Go to frame 25 and paste again: the first contact once more, so the cycle closes.
  4. Add the in-between poses: the passing positions on frames 7 and 19, with the body a little higher.
  5. Pin the planted foot on each contact frame and release it when it lifts ().
  6. Select the layer, tick Repeat frames from 1 to 25, and tick Keep moving. Pip now walks across the whole scene.

Walking on the spot. For a character who stays in the middle while the world moves past, leave Keep moving off and scroll the background instead (). It's the treadmill trick of old cartoons.

Every scene has a camera. At first it simply looks at the whole scene, but you can move it on the timeline like any part: slowly push in on a face, pan across a landscape, or tilt the picture for a dizzy moment.

Imagine a real film set. The actors stand on the stage, and the camera operator moves the camera around them. The camera never pushes an actor. It's the same in Nyahmation: the camera only changes what you see. Characters, poses and pins stay exactly where they are on the stage, so a pinned foot never slides because the camera moved.

Moving the camera

  1. In Animate mode, choose the Camera tool (C) and go to the frame where the move should end.
  2. Drag on the canvas to pan. The picture follows the mouse, as if you were sliding the stage under the camera.
  3. ⌥-drag up or down to zoom in or out, and ⇧-drag to turn the picture around its middle.
  4. Play it. The camera glides there from where it was, because its first move also keeps its starting place on frame 1.

Camera moves are poses like any other: they appear on the Camera row, can be dragged to retime them, and use the same easing (an Ease in and out camera move feels like a smooth dolly). Dragging on the Scene row retimes them together with everything else.

With the Camera tool chosen, or the Camera row selected, Properties shows the camera on this frame:

Camera view on or off

Camera view (on the timeline bar, on by default) shows the picture exactly as the video will. Turn it off to see the whole stage, with the camera's frame drawn on it in orange. That's handy when a character is about to walk into the shot and you need to pose it while it's still out of the picture. All the tools work either way.

Pictures and zoom. Zooming in enlarges everything, including PNG pictures. The export window takes the camera's closest zoom into account when it warns about pictures that may look soft.

Once there's a camera, backgrounds can do much more than sit there. Each layer's Properties have a Camera and scrolling section.

Depth: near things move more

Look out of a train window: the fence next to the track whizzes past, the trees go by more slowly, and the mountains hardly move. That difference, called parallax, is how our eyes judge distance, and it makes a flat cartoon feel deep.

Give each background layer a Depth. 1 is the stage, where the characters are. Less than 1 is further away and moves less when the camera pans or zooms: try 0.3 for far hills and 0.6 for nearer ones. More than 1 is foreground and moves more: 1.5 for a bush right in front of the camera. Depth only shows when the camera moves, so add a camera pan to see it.

A sky behind everything

Press Esc to see the scene's Properties and tick Sky: Gradient. Pick the top and bottom colours. The sky fills the whole picture, fading from top to bottom, and stays put whatever the camera does, like a real sky.

Fixed to the camera: titles and corners

Tick Fixed to the camera for a layer that should stay in the same place on screen, at the same size, whatever the camera does: a title, a logo, a narrator in the corner. It's like a sticker on the camera's lens.

Following a part: tags and speech bubbles

A fixed layer can also follow a part in another layer. It rides along with the part but keeps its own size and angle, so a name tag stays readable and a speech bubble stays level when the head tilts or the camera zooms in.

  1. Make a new background layer (+ Background) and, in Build mode, draw the bubble or tag where it should sit next to the character.
  2. Select the layer, tick Fixed to the camera, and choose the part in Follows (the head, say).

When the camera zooms in, the bubble stays the same size on screen, and its distance from the head grows with the head, so it never ends up on top of the face.

Scrolling scenery

Scroll slides a layer sideways as the scene plays, in pixels a second; negative numbers go left. Tick Repeat sideways and copies of the layer are laid side by side, so it never runs out. Use it for clouds drifting by, scenery passing a car window, or the ground under a character walking on the spot.

Treadmill walk. Put the character in the middle with a walk cycle (without Keep moving), and scroll the ground and scenery at walking speed, the far layers more slowly than the near ones.

Background layers saved to the library keep their depth, scrolling and fixed setting. (A "follows" link isn't kept, because the part it named belongs to another project.)

Effects add the finishing touches: a shadow to lift a character off the background, a glow for magic, haze to push far hills into the distance.

Adding an effect

Select a part (its Properties have an Effects section) or a layer's row (Layer effects, for the whole layer), and choose from Add an effect…. × removes one.

Effects apply to the whole group

A shadow on a character's layer casts one shadow of the whole character, the way a real shadow works. If every part cast its own, the shadows would double up and look darker wherever parts overlap, such as the arm over the body. In the same way, an effect on a group (an arm with its forearm and hand) applies to the group as one picture.

Animating effects

In Animate mode, changing an effect's number records a pose, like moving a part: a glow can pulse, a shadow can lengthen as the sun sets, a blur can sharpen as something comes into focus. The marks appear on the part's row and retime like any other. Colours don't animate.

Clipping: pupils that stay in the eye

Tick Clip (“Parts inside only show on this shape”) on a shape, and the parts inside it only show where the shape itself is. Put the pupil inside the eye white and clip: the pupil can look all the way to the side and never pokes out of the eye. It works for shading inside a body, a pattern on a shirt, or a face in a window.

Speed. Blur and big glows are the slowest things Nyahmation draws. Parts that don't change from one frame to the next (most scenery) are drawn once and reused, so they cost little. If playback stutters, lower the Preview quality (); exports are always full quality. Onion skins leave effects out, to stay quick.

Add a voice recording, sound effects or music. Nyahmation plays it in time with the picture and puts it in the exported video.

Adding a sound

  1. In Animate mode, go to the frame where the sound should start.
  2. Choose File → Import Art or Sound… (⌘I) and pick a sound file: WAV, MP3, M4A, AAC, OGG or FLAC.
  3. It appears on the Sound row with its waveform (the wiggly shape of the sound: tall where it's loud, flat where it's quiet). If the sound is longer than the scene, the scene gets longer to fit it.

Working with sounds

  • Move a sound by dragging it along the Sound row.
  • Click it to see its properties: name, start frame, volume (up to 200%) and mute. Press Delete to remove it.
  • Sound while scrubbing (a checkbox on the timeline, on by default) plays a tiny slice of sound for each frame as you step with the arrow keys or drag along the number row. This is how you find exactly where each word starts.
  • During playback the picture follows the sound, so they never drift apart, even on a slow computer.

Recording tip. Record dialogue with your phone's voice memo app or QuickTime Player (File → New Audio Recording), in a quiet room, and save or share it to the Mac. Speak a little more clearly and slowly than normal; it makes lip sync easier, for you and for Auto lip sync.

A talking character swaps between a handful of mouth drawings, one for each group of sounds. In Nyahmation the mouth is a switch layer: a part that shows one drawing at a time. You can type the letter of each mouth shape as you hear the dialogue, or let Nyahmation listen and fill them in for you.

The nine mouth shapes

These are the classic cartoon mouth shapes. Draw one of each for your character (you can start with just A, C, D and X and add the rest later).

Making a mouth

  1. In Build mode, draw each mouth shape. A mouth can be several shapes (lips, teeth, tongue); group each mouth's pieces with ⌘G.
  2. Name each one after its letter (double-click the name in the Layers panel): A, C, D, X… Names like rest, MBP, FV or mouth_D work too.
  3. Select all the mouth shapes and choose Object → Make Switch Layer (⇧⌘M). They become one part called Mouth.
  4. In the Layers panel, drag the Mouth onto the head, so it moves with the head. Use the Joints tool to place its joint in the middle of the mouth.

Using PNG pictures instead? Name the files A.png, D.png and so on, select the Mouth, and click Add drawings from files… in Properties. Each file lands on its letter.

Properties also lists any of the nine shapes that are still missing. Clicking a drawing in Build mode makes it the rest mouth: the one shown when nothing is animated.

Automatic lip sync

Nyahmation can listen to the dialogue and fill in the mouth shapes for you, using Rhubarb Lip Sync. It's a great first draft: it gets most shapes right, and you spend your time polishing instead of typing every letter.

  1. Import the dialogue () and switch to Animate mode.
  2. Click the Mouth row's name on the timeline. The mouth palette appears; click Auto lip sync…
  3. Pick the sound, the language, and if you can, type the words. Click Start.
  4. After a few seconds to a minute, the Mouth row fills with shapes for the length of the sound. Play it, and fix any shape you don't like by typing its letter (below).
  • Anything the mouth already did during the sound is replaced; before and after it is kept. It's one step to undo.
  • If the mouth has no drawing for a shape, the nearest one it does have stands in, so an A-to-F set still moves.
  • If the Auto lip sync window says it isn't installed yet, see .

Lip syncing by hand

  1. Import the dialogue and switch to Animate mode.
  2. Click the Mouth row's name on the timeline. The mouth palette appears above the timeline.
  3. Go to the frame where the first word starts. Step with → and listen: you'll hear each frame.
  4. Type the letter of the mouth shape for the sound you hear. The mouth changes on this frame and the playhead moves on one frame, so you can keep listening and typing.
  5. If the sound hasn't changed, just press → to move on. A mouth stays until the next letter you type.
  6. Made a mistake? Backspace steps back and clears that frame.
  7. Press Space to watch it with the sound.

To retime one mouth, drag the left edge of its block on the Mouth row (⇧-drag moves the ones after it too).

Tips from animators. Mouths usually look best a frame or two before the sound. To nudge a whole phrase, select its ◆ marks on the Mouth row and ⇧-drag. Don't try to show every letter: hit the big, visible sounds (M/B/P closing, wide vowels) and let the rest go.

Letters beat tool keys. While the Mouth is selected in Animate mode, the letter keys set mouth shapes (so H sets the H mouth instead of choosing the Hand tool). Press Esc to deselect the Mouth and get the tool keys back.

Other switch layers

Switch layers aren't just for mouths: make one for eyes (open, half, closed, for blinks), hands (fist, open, pointing) or eyebrows. If the drawings aren't named after mouth letters, press 1–9 to pick them, or click them in the palette. The playhead doesn't move on for these.

A character's frames remember letters, not pictures. You can redraw a mouth, or swap in a different drawing set with Drawing set, and all the lip sync follows.

  1. Choose File → Export Video… (⌘E), or click Export… on the timeline.
  2. Pick a format, a size and which frames.
  3. Click Export… and choose where to save. A progress bar shows each frame; Cancel stops it.

Which format?

FormatBest for
MP4 video (H.264)Watching and sharing: plays on phones, computers, YouTube and messaging apps. Includes the sound. If in doubt, pick this one.
MP4 video (H.265)The same quality in a smaller file. Plays on recent Macs, iPhones and most new devices; older computers may not play it.
WebM video (VP9)Web pages. Can have a see-through background, to put a character over a web page or other footage.
MOV (ProRes 4444)Video editing apps (Final Cut Pro, Premiere, DaVinci Resolve). Top quality, very big files, optional see-through background, and the sound uncompressed.

  • Size: the scene size, or 720p up to 4K. Bigger is sharper but takes longer and makes a bigger file. 1080p is a good choice.
  • Frames: the whole scene, or only the loop range you set with I and O.
  • See-through leaves out the scene's background colour and sky, so only the characters and scenery are drawn.
  • Every frame is drawn at full quality with all its effects, whatever the Preview setting; it just takes a little longer on a slow computer. The video is shot through the camera.
  • If a PNG picture in the scene will be shown bigger than its own pixels (including when the camera zooms in), the Export window lists it, because it may look soft. Pick a smaller size, or use a bigger picture.

Projects

A project is one scene, saved as one .nyah file. The file holds everything: the drawings, the animation, the camera, pictures and sounds, so you can copy it to another computer or back it up in one piece.

  • Save (⌘S) and Save As… (⇧⌘S). A dot after the project's name means there are unsaved changes.
  • Nyahmation asks before closing, or opening another project, if there are unsaved changes.
  • Saving is safe: the new file is written completely before it replaces the old one, so a crash can't damage your project.
  • Double-click a .nyah file in Finder, or drop it on Nyahmation's Dock icon, to open it.

Autosave: a safety net

While a project has unsaved changes, Nyahmation quietly keeps a copy of it, about once a minute, in its own folder. It doesn't replace your file; it's a safety net under it, in case the computer crashes, the battery dies, or the app is forced to quit. When you save, open another project or discard your changes, the copy is deleted.

If Nyahmation didn't close properly last time, it says so when it starts, with the name of the work and when it was last copied:

ButtonWhat happens
RestoreOpens the work as unsaved changes to its original file. Press Save to put it back where it came from.
DeleteThrows the copy away.
Decide laterKeeps the copy and asks again the next time Nyahmation starts.

Still save often. Autosave keeps at most a minute's work at risk, but only ⌘S updates your own file.

The library folder

Library items are .nyahitem files in Documents/Nyahmation Library. They are ordinary files: you can back them up, share them, or organise them in folders.
